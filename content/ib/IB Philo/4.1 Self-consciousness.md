>Personhood = what makes someone a _“person”_ rather than just a body or some kind of matter.

>Self-consciousness = the ability to **reflect on yourself**, know “this is me,” and think about your own thoughts.

## Main Questions

- Do you need self-consciousness to be a person?
- Is personhood about rationality, memory, experiences, or social identity?

> [!tip] Quick summary
> - **Kant** - rational mind
> - **Locke** - your memories make “you”
> - **Hume** - self = experiences, no core
> - **Korsgaard** - acting on your values + community
## Philosophers

### 1. Immanuel Kant
==Rationality and Autonomy==
- Personhood = **rationality + autonomy**.
- You’re a person if you can **make moral decisions for yourself**, not just follow instincts.
- Self-consciousness = crucial, because you need to see yourself as an autonomous agent capable of ethics.

**Main counter-argument:**\
Kant’s ideas don’t include infants and ppl with cognitive impairments, or other non-rational beings


### 2. John Locke
==Psychological Continuity==
- you are your memories
- you are a person because you remember past experiences –> recognize yourself over time (continuity)
- for this, self-consciousness is necessary

**Main counter-argument**:\
Memory can be faulty, e.g. loose memories in an accident etc.\
Are you then not the same person if you forget things?\
*note to self:* this argument seems valid but also kinda nit-picky


### 3. David Hume
==Bundle Theory==
- Self = **bundle of perceptions** / experiences
- There is **no fixed “core self”**, only a constantly changing collection of experiences.
- Self-consciousness –> noticing your experiences, but it doesn’t mean there’s a stable identity.

**Main counterargument:**\
People feel continuity over time. Hume can’t fully explain why we _feel_ like the same person from childhood to now. There must be something else

### 4. Christine Korsgaard
==Practical Identity==
- Personhood = **reflecting on who you are and acting on your values**.
- Self-consciousness = seeing yourself as part of groups, communities, and making moral choices accordingly.

- Social/cultural influences might make “identity” messy.
- Can’t always cleanly separate individual choices from society’s impact.
